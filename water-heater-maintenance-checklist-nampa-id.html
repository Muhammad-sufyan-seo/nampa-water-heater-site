Two Lists: What You Can Watch For, and What a Technician Handles

A water heater maintenance checklist splits into two parts: observation-only checks that any Nampa homeowner can do safely, and hands-on inspection points that require a qualified professional. The dividing line is simple. If a check means looking at the unit or listening to it, it is on your list. If it means opening a panel, operating a valve, breaking a seal, draining water, or working near the gas or electrical supply, it is on ours.

This page explains what gets checked and why. It is not a do-it-yourself maintenance guide, and it deliberately leaves out step-by-step instructions for the professional-only items. When you're ready to book, our water heater maintenance in Nampa, Idaho page covers scheduling and pricing.

How Often a Water Heater Needs Maintenance

The maintenance interval for your water heater is set by its manufacturer, and the owner's manual governs the exact schedule for your unit. Intervals differ between brands, between models, and between tank, tankless, and heat pump designs, so we don't put a single universal number on this page. Follow the maintenance schedule specified by the manufacturer, and if you no longer have the manual, the model number on the rating plate lets you look it up or lets us look it up for you.

The observation checks in Part 1 below are different: they carry no risk, so there is no reason not to glance at your water heater whenever you're in the garage, basement, or utility closet where it sits.

Part 1: Homeowner-Safe Checks (Look and Listen Only)

Six checks are safe for a homeowner because none of them involve touching a valve, opening a panel, or changing a setting. Do them with the water heater running normally, and keep your hands off the pipes at the top of the unit, which can be hot.

  1. Look for rust, corrosion, or moisture on the tank and around its base. Check the tank jacket, the floor beneath it, and the drain pan if your unit sits in one. Water or rust-colored staining at the base is worth a call; water seeping from the tank body itself is the most serious version of this sign. Our water heater leaking in Nampa, Idaho guide explains how leak sources differ.
  2. Look for drips at the visible connections. With your eyes only, check the water pipes entering and leaving the top of the unit, the drain valve near the bottom, and the open end of the discharge pipe that runs down the side of the tank from the temperature and pressure relief valve. Moisture, drips, or a white or green mineral crust at any of these points is a sign to note and report. Do not tighten, open, or lift anything.
  3. Confirm the area around the unit is clear. Keep boxes, laundry, cleaning supplies, paint, gasoline, and other stored items away from the water heater. On a gas unit this matters most: the burner needs an unobstructed supply of combustion air, and manufacturers warn against storing or using flammable liquids or vapors near a gas appliance. The required clearances differ by model, so the owner's manual or the label on the unit lists the exact distances for yours.
  4. Listen while the unit heats. Popping, rumbling, or crackling from a tank water heater usually points to a layer of mineral sediment on the tank floor. Note when you hear it and how often. Our water heater making noise guide covers what different sounds can mean.
  5. Note the temperature setting, without changing it. On a gas tank unit, the setting is usually marked on the dial of the gas control on the front of the tank; on a tankless or heat pump unit with a display, it shows on the screen. Write down what it reads. On an electric tank water heater, the thermostats sit behind access panels next to live high-voltage wiring, so leave those panels closed and let the technician check the setting during the visit. Do not raise or lower the setting yourself; a higher setting increases scalding risk, and any change should follow the manufacturer's instructions for your unit.
  6. Record the model and serial number from the rating plate. The rating plate on the side of the unit lists both numbers. Many manufacturers encode the manufacture date in the serial number, though the format differs by brand. The date tells you and us how old the unit is; our water heater lifespan guide explains what age does and doesn't tell you about a unit's condition.
Stop and call if you notice any of these Water coming from the tank body itself, a discharge pipe that is dripping or running, soot or scorch marks on the outside of a gas unit, a burner access cover that is missing or out of place, or a breaker that keeps tripping on an electric unit. If you smell gas in the air near the water heater, leave the home first and call your gas utility or 911 from outside, as our water heater rotten egg smell guide describes.

Do not remove the anode rod, open the drain valve, lift the relief valve lever, remove the burner cover or element access panels, or adjust the gas control or thermostat yourself. Each of those steps carries a scald, pressure, gas, or electrical hazard, and Part 2 explains which.

Part 2: Professional-Only Inspection Points (and Why)

Six inspection points on a water heater require a qualified professional: the anode rod, the temperature and pressure relief valve, tank flushing and sediment removal, the gas control, burner, and venting, electrical components, and the gas and electrical supply connections. For each one below, we name what gets checked and the specific hazard that makes it a job for a technician rather than a homeowner.

Anode Rod Inspection and Replacement

The anode rod is checked by removing it from a pressurized tank, which means breaking a threaded seal on a vessel full of hot water under household water pressure. The rod is a sacrificial magnesium or aluminum rod that corrodes in place of the tank steel, and the only way to see how much of it is left is to pull it out. Done incorrectly, it can release hot water under pressure or leave a fitting that leaks afterward. Any change to the anode rod, including a switch to a different rod type, should follow the manufacturer's instructions for that unit. If you've had a sulfur odor in your hot water, our rotten egg smell guide explains how the anode rod can be involved.

Temperature and Pressure Relief (T&P) Valve Testing

Testing the T&P valve means deliberately triggering a safety device that discharges scalding-hot water under pressure. If the discharge pipe is missing, loose, or poorly routed, that water can spray toward whoever is standing nearby. An older valve that hasn't been operated in years can also fail to close again and keep leaking once it has been opened. A technician checks the discharge piping before the test, is ready for a valve that doesn't reseat, and can replace a valve that fails the test.

Tank Flushing and Sediment Removal

Flushing a tank means draining hot, pressurized water and managing the unit's heat source while it's empty. The water is hot enough to scald, the drain valve on an older unit may not reseal properly once opened, and the drained water has to go somewhere safe. On a gas unit, the gas control has to be managed through the process and the burner or pilot relit correctly afterward. On an electric unit, the power has to be handled correctly before, during, and after the tank is drained and refilled. These are the reasons we don't publish flushing steps.

Gas Control, Burner, and Venting Inspection

Gas-side inspection is a combustion-safety check, covering the gas control, the burner and pilot assembly, the flame sensing components, and the vent that carries exhaust out of the home. A burner that isn't burning cleanly, or a vent that isn't drawing exhaust out properly, can put carbon monoxide into the living space. This work involves the gas supply and the combustion chamber, and it belongs to a qualified technician.

Electrical Component Testing on Electric Units

Electric tank water heaters run on a high-voltage circuit, and the heating elements, thermostats, and high-limit switch sit behind access panels right next to that wiring. Testing them carries a real shock risk: the circuit has to be correctly de-energized and confirmed dead before anything is touched, and some tests require trained use of meters around electrical components. Leave the access panels closed and let a technician handle every step of this inspection.

Gas Line and Electrical Supply to the Unit

Anything involving the gas line or the electrical supply feeding the water heater is professional-only work. That covers the gas shutoff, connector, and fittings on a gas unit, and the circuit, disconnect, and wiring connections on an electric unit. A mistake at either point is a gas-leak, fire, or shock hazard rather than a water problem.

What a Technician Checks, by Water Heater Type

The professional inspection points change with the type of water heater. The table below lists what we look at on each of the four residential types we service, beyond the tank or cabinet exterior and the water connections, which we check on every unit.

Water Heater TypeInspection Points Specific to This Type
Gas tankAnode rod, T&P valve and discharge pipe, sediment, drain valve, gas control, burner and pilot assembly, thermocouple or flame sensor, venting and combustion air supply
Electric tankAnode rod, T&P valve and discharge pipe, sediment, drain valve, upper and lower heating elements, thermostats, high-limit switch, wiring and circuit condition
Tankless (gas or electric)Heat exchanger scale, inlet water filter, error code history, ignition system or heating elements, venting on gas models, condensate drain on condensing models, relief valve
Heat pump (hybrid)Air filter, condensate drain, fan and airflow around the unit, anode rod, T&P valve and discharge pipe, sediment, backup heating elements

On any home with a closed plumbing system, meaning one with a check valve or pressure reducing valve on the incoming water line, we also check whether an expansion tank is present and still working, since thermal expansion pressure that has nowhere to go ends up on the water heater tank and its relief valve. Where a unit has seismic straps or a drain pan, we confirm they're still in place and intact.

We service water heaters from Rheem, A.O. Smith, Bradford White, American Standard, Navien, Rinnai, Noritz, and State Water Heaters, and we follow each manufacturer's instructions for the model in front of us.

What Happens After the Inspection

After the inspection, we explain what we found and what, if anything, needs attention. Most findings fall into one of three outcomes:

  • Unit in sound condition — The maintenance items are completed and you're given the next interval to plan for, based on your unit's owner's manual.
  • Worn or failed part on a sound unit — A depleted anode rod, a T&P valve that won't reseat, a failing heating element, or a worn thermocouple is a repair. Our water heater repair in Nampa, Idaho service covers these.
  • Tank or heat exchanger at the end of its life — A tank that has corroded through cannot be repaired. Our repair vs. replace guide lays out how we weigh that decision with you.
