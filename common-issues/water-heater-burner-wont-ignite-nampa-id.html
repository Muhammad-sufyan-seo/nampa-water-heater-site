First: Does It Never Light, or Does It Light and Then Go Out?

These are two different failures with two different causes. If your water heater's pilot or burner lights successfully and then goes out — within seconds, minutes, or hours — that's a flame-retention problem, and our pilot light won't stay lit guide covers it, including the worn thermocouple that causes it most often. This page covers the opposite problem: attempting to light the water heater and getting no flame at all, on any attempt.

Common Causes of a Burner That Won't Ignite At All

Three types of failure stop a burner from ever producing a flame, each pointing to a different part of the ignition system:

  • No Spark From the Igniter — Both piezo (push-button) and electronic igniters create a spark at an electrode positioned next to the burner. If that spark never happens — no click, no visible spark, nothing — the igniter itself has failed, or its electrode has become fouled or knocked out of position so the spark can't reach the gas. Without a spark, the burner has no ignition source no matter how much gas is present.
  • Gas Not Reaching the Burner at the Moment of Ignition — An igniter can spark correctly and still produce no flame if gas isn't actually present at the burner when the spark fires. The gas supply valve may be closed, sometimes accidentally during other work near the unit, or, after a water heater is serviced or newly installed, air trapped in the gas line can keep gas from reaching the burner until the line is fully purged. In both cases the ignition attempt itself looks normal; the burner just has nothing to ignite.
  • An Upstream Safety Interlock Blocking the Ignition Signal — Some water heaters include a safety switch, such as a blocked-flue or draft safety switch, that has to confirm safe venting conditions before the control allows an ignition attempt to happen at all. If that interlock doesn't close, the control never sends a signal to the igniter or the gas valve, so from the outside it looks like the unit simply isn't trying to light.

Safe Homeowner Diagnostic Checks

  1. Watch and listen during an attempt. Follow the manufacturer's lighting instructions and pay attention to whether you hear or see the igniter click or spark at all — that tells you whether the problem is likely at the igniter or further downstream.
  2. Check the gas supply valve. Confirm the valve on the gas line to the water heater is fully open, with the handle running parallel to the pipe.
  3. Note whether the unit is newly installed or recently serviced. If so, trapped air in the line is a reasonable explanation, and the manufacturer's instructions may call for a specific number of ignition attempts to purge it.
  4. Look for any indicator light or lockout signal on units that have one, without attempting to clear it yourself.
  5. Stop after a couple of attempts. Repeating the ignition sequence many times in a row doesn't diagnose the cause and isn't the safe way to troubleshoot it.

If you smell gas at any point during an attempt, stop immediately and treat it as a possible gas leak rather than an ignition problem.

When to Call a Professional

  • The igniter never clicks or sparks on any attempt
  • The igniter sparks normally but the burner still produces no flame
  • The unit was recently installed or serviced and still won't ignite after a few attempts
  • You notice an indicator light or lockout signal you don't recognize
  • You smell gas during an attempt — stop and treat it as a possible leak

Burner Won't Ignite FAQs

A burner that never produces a flame, on any attempt, is an ignition failure rather than a flame-retention problem. The usual causes are an igniter that isn't producing a spark at all, gas that isn't actually reaching the burner when the spark fires — from a closed supply valve or air trapped in the line after service or a new installation — or a safety interlock, such as a blocked-flue or draft safety switch where the unit has one, that keeps the control from sending an ignition signal in the first place.

A burner that won't ignite at all never produces a flame on any attempt, which points to the igniter, the gas supply at the moment of ignition, or a safety interlock. A pilot that lights and then goes out is a different problem — a flame-retention failure, most often caused by a worn thermocouple — covered separately on our Pilot Light Won't Stay Lit guide.

That points to the gas side rather than the ignition side: the spark is working, but gas isn't present at the burner to catch it.
