Water Heater Smells Like Rotten Eggs in Nampa, Idaho

A rotten-egg smell around a water heater in Nampa is one of two very different problems: a possible gas leak, or a sulfur odor in the hot water itself. The first is an emergency. The second is a water-chemistry issue inside the tank. Here's how to tell them apart and what to do about each.

First: Is It a Gas Smell or a Hot Water Smell?

Where you notice the smell decides what you do next. A rotten-egg odor in the air near the water heater is treated as a possible gas leak. A sulfur odor that appears only when you run hot water from a tap is a water problem inside the tank, not a gas leak.

1. Smell in the air near the unit: treat it as a possible gas leak

Natural gas and propane have no smell of their own, so suppliers add an odorant (typically a mercaptan compound) that smells like rotten eggs. If you notice that smell in the air around a gas water heater, near any gas appliance, or anywhere else in your home, it can mean gas is escaping. This is an emergency, and it is a job for the gas utility and emergency services first, not a water heater company.

If you smell gas, do this now:
  1. Leave the building right away, taking everyone, including pets, with you.
  2. Do not turn any light switches or appliances on or off, do not unplug anything, and do not use a phone, lighter, match, or anything that could make a spark while you are inside.
  3. Do not try to find the leak, adjust the water heater's gas control, or relight a pilot light.
  4. Once you are outside and well away from the building, call your natural gas utility's emergency line (or your propane supplier) or 911.
  5. Stay outside until the utility or emergency responders tell you it is safe to go back in.
If you can't tell whether the smell is in the air or in the water, treat it as a gas leak.

Once the gas utility has checked the home and cleared it, call us at (208) 987-5152 if the water heater itself needs gas water heater repair in Nampa, Idaho. We follow the same safety-first order on our water heater leaking in Nampa, Idaho guide: make the home safe first, then diagnose the unit.

2. Smell only in the hot water from the tap: a water-chemistry problem

If the odor shows up when you run hot water, and the cold water from the same faucet smells normal, the source points to the inside of the water heater tank rather than a gas line. The smell is usually described as sulfur or rotten eggs and comes from hydrogen sulfide gas dissolved in the hot water. It is unpleasant, but it is not a gas leak, and the rest of this page covers what causes it and how it gets fixed.

If your cold water also has a mild odor, the source may be your water supply rather than the tank. In that case, the water heater is passing along an odor that is already in the water coming into the house.

Possible Causes of Sulfur-Smelling Hot Water

A rotten-egg smell in hot water has more than one possible cause, and the right fix depends on which one is at work in your tank. These are the three possibilities we check for on Nampa service calls:

  • Anode rod reaction with sulfate-reducing bacteria — Tank water heaters contain a sacrificial anode rod, often made of magnesium, that corrodes in place of the steel tank. In some water chemistries, sulfate-reducing bacteria in the tank react with the hydrogen the anode rod releases and produce hydrogen sulfide, the rotten-egg odor. Swapping a magnesium rod for a manufacturer-approved aluminum/zinc-alloy or powered anode rod is a known way to reduce this reaction.
  • Bacterial growth in an idle or low-temperature tank — A water heater that has sat unused for a while (a vacation home, a stretch away from home, an unused second unit) or one set to a low temperature gives bacteria a warm, still environment to grow in. If the odor first appeared when you ran hot water after an idle period, tell the technician.
  • Sulfur already present in the water supply — Some water sources carry sulfur compounds before the water ever reaches the tank. If the cold water also smells faintly, the supply is the more likely source, and the water heater is not the root cause.

More than one of these can be happening at once, which is why we confirm the source before replacing parts. A new anode rod does not fix an odor that is coming from the water supply.

Safe Homeowner Diagnostic Checks

These checks help narrow down the source without opening, draining, or adjusting the water heater:

  1. Confirm it is not a gas smell. Stand near the water heater with no water running. If you smell rotten eggs in the air, stop here and follow the gas-leak steps above.
  2. Compare hot and cold water at the same faucet. Run the cold side into a clean glass, then the hot side into another, and smell each. Odor only in the hot glass points to the tank; odor in both points to the water supply.
  3. Check more than one faucet. If the hot water smells at every fixture in the house, the source is the water heater or the supply rather than a single faucet or drain.
  4. Note whether the drain is the real source. A sulfur smell from a sink drain can be mistaken for smelly water. Fill a glass and step away from the sink before smelling it.
  5. Think about recent history. Has the water heater sat unused, had its temperature turned down, or had its anode rod replaced recently? Each of these is useful information for the technician.

Do not remove the anode rod, raise the thermostat setting, or add chemicals to the tank yourself. Higher water temperatures increase scalding risk, and changes to the anode rod or temperature should follow the manufacturer's instructions for your specific unit.
