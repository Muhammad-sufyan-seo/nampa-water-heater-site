Tank and tankless water heaters are sized two different ways. Water Heater Sizing in Nampa: Two Methods, Not One Number

The right water heater size for a Nampa home depends on which type of unit you're buying, because tank and tankless water heaters are sized by different measurements. Homes across 83651, 83686, and 83687 range from one-bathroom houses to multi-bathroom homes with soaking tubs and several people getting ready at once, and no single gallon count or flow figure fits all of them.

That's why you won't find a "for this many people, buy this size" chart on this page. A chart like that ignores the two things that actually decide the answer: how your household uses hot water at its busiest, and the published performance of the specific model you're considering. This guide explains both methods so you can walk into an installation conversation knowing what questions to ask.

Why Tank and Tankless Water Heaters Are Sized Differently

A tank water heater stores heated water and reheats the tank as you draw from it, so it is sized by how much hot water it can deliver over a busy stretch of time. A tankless water heater stores nothing and heats water as it flows through a heat exchanger, so it is sized by how much water it can heat at once. The table below sets the two methods side by side.

Heat pump (hybrid) water heaters store water in a tank, so they follow the tank method. If you're still deciding between a tank and a tankless unit, our tankless vs. tank water heater comparison covers the trade-offs beyond sizing.

How to Size a Tank Water Heater: First Hour Rating and Peak Demand

A tank water heater is sized by matching the unit's First Hour Rating to the hot water your household uses in its single busiest hour. Average daily use matters less than that peak, because the peak is when a tank runs out.

Stored Volume Plus Recovery

First Hour Rating (FHR) is an industry rating for storage water heaters that estimates how much hot water the unit can supply in an hour that starts with a fully heated tank. The rating counts two things together: the hot water already stored in the tank, and the incoming cold water the burner or heating elements can bring up to temperature during that hour. The manufacturer lists the First Hour Rating in the specifications for each model.

Why Tank Capacity Alone Doesn't Settle the Size

The gallon capacity printed on a tank tells you how much water it holds, not how much hot water it delivers in a busy hour. Recovery depends on the heat source and its input rating, so two tanks of the same nominal capacity, from different product lines or with different fuel types, can publish different First Hour Ratings. Compare the First Hour Rating of each model you're considering, not just the tank size. Fuel type is part of that comparison; our gas vs. electric water heater comparison explains how the two heat sources differ.

How to Map Your Household's Busiest Hour

Your busiest hour is the stretch of the day when hot water uses pile up, and four steps identify it:

  1. Pick the hour. It might be the morning rush, the evening routine, or a weekend stretch, and it can differ between school days and days off.
  2. List every hot water use in that hour. Count each shower, bath, load of laundry on a warm or hot cycle, dishwasher run, and sink task that happens within it.
  3. Mark which uses overlap. Back-to-back showers while the dishwasher runs draw down a tank far harder than the same uses spread through the day.
  4. Compare against published ratings. Look up the First Hour Rating of your current unit and any replacement you're considering, and ask whether it covers that hour with room to spare.

If your current tank already runs out during that hour, the replacement needs a higher First Hour Rating, a change in routine, or both. If it never runs out, matching its rating is a reasonable starting point.

How to Size a Tankless Water Heater: Flow Rate and Temperature Rise

A tankless water heater is sized by the combined flow rate of the fixtures you run at the same time and the temperature rise needed to heat your incoming water to the set temperature. Both numbers have to fit within what the unit can deliver together.

Flow Rate: How Many Fixtures Run at Once

Flow rate is measured in gallons per minute (GPM). Each shower, faucet, and appliance draws its own flow, and a tankless unit has to supply the total of everything open at the same moment. Two showers running together is a different sizing problem from one shower followed by another. Your fixtures' rated flow is listed in their product documentation or packaging, and the realistic list is the fixtures your household actually opens together, not every fixture in the house.

Temperature Rise: How Cold the Incoming Water Is

Temperature rise is the difference between the temperature of the water entering the unit and the temperature you want at the tap. The colder the incoming water, the larger the rise, and the less flow a given tankless unit can heat to full temperature. Manufacturer specifications show how a model's output changes across different temperature rises, so the right comparison uses the rise your home needs when its incoming water is at its coldest, not when it is warmest.

When One Tankless Unit Isn't Enough

Every tankless model has a maximum flow it can heat at a given temperature rise. If your household regularly runs more fixtures at once than a single unit can handle at your temperature rise, the options are a larger unit or, in some homes, more than one unit, either linked together or placed near the fixtures they serve. The gas supply, venting, and electrical capacity at the install location also limit what can be installed, which is why those get checked as part of sizing. Our tankless water heater installation service covers that on-site work once the size is settled.

What Undersizing Looks Like Day to Day

An undersized water heater shows itself at the busiest moments, and the symptom depends on the type.

An Undersized Tank Runs Out

An undersized tank delivers full-temperature water until its stored hot water is used up, then the water cools and the household waits while the tank reheats. The last shower of the morning goes cold, or the dishwasher and a shower can't both finish hot. Sediment taking up space in the tank or a failing heating component can cause the same pattern on a unit that used to keep up, so a tank that has started running out sooner than it once did is worth inspecting before replacing.

An Undersized Tankless Unit Falls Behind on Flow

An undersized tankless unit doesn't run out; it falls behind. When the combined flow of open fixtures exceeds what the unit can heat at your temperature rise, the water arrives cooler than the set temperature, or, on models that restrict flow to hold temperature, the pressure at the fixtures drops. One shower runs fine, and the problem appears when a second fixture opens. If those symptoms show up on a unit that used to keep up, see our tankless water heater repair page, since scale and clogged filters cause similar behavior.

The Hidden Costs of Going Too Big

Oversizing buys capacity your household never uses, and you pay for it up front and, with a tank, every day after. A larger tank keeps more water hot around the clock, so it has more stored heat to lose between uses, and it takes up more floor space in a garage or utility closet. A larger tankless unit doesn't hold standby water, but it costs more and can require a larger gas line, different venting, or electrical upgrades that a correctly sized unit wouldn't. The right size covers your actual peak with sensible headroom and stops there.

Household Changes That Change Your Sizing Answer

Five changes are worth raising before you replace a water heater, because each one shifts peak demand:

  • Adding a bathroom — More showers available means more chances for them to run at once.
  • Installing a large soaking tub — Filling a big tub draws a lot of hot water in a short window, which matters for both tank and tankless sizing.
  • Growing the household — More people getting ready on the same schedule stacks up the busiest hour.
  • Shifting routines — Moving from staggered showers to everyone showering in the same window changes peak demand without changing headcount.
  • Switching unit types — Going from tank to tankless means your old tank's size no longer tells you anything; the new unit is sized by flow rate and temperature rise instead.

What We Assess Before Recommending a Size

Sizing a water heater correctly takes information only your home can supply, which is why we size units on site rather than from a chart. On a sizing visit, our technician looks at six things:

  1. Fixture count — every shower, tub, faucet, and appliance that uses hot water.
  2. Simultaneous use — which of those fixtures your household actually runs at the same time.
  3. Your current unit — the model and serial number on the data plate, and whether it has been keeping up.
  4. Household routine — when the busiest hour happens and what goes on during it.
  5. Incoming water — the temperature rise a tankless unit would need to cover.
  6. Install location limits — space, venting, gas supply, and electrical capacity where the unit will go.

From there we compare published First Hour Ratings or flow-rate specifications for the models that fit, and recommend a size you can check against the manufacturer's own numbers.
